Why Nigerians Have Normalized Suffering

October 22, 2026

There is something deeply disturbing about the way Nigerians have learned to live with suffering. What should outrage us has gradually become ordinary. What should be considered unacceptable is often dismissed with a simple phrase: “That is Nigeria.”

Nigerians queue for hours to get basic services, struggle with rising food prices, deal with unreliable electricity, poor roads, unemployment and an increasingly expensive cost of living. Yet instead of demanding better conditions, many people have become experts at adjusting to them.

We celebrate survival so much that we sometimes forget to question why survival has become the standard.

There is nothing wrong with resilience. In fact, resilience is one of the strongest qualities Nigerians possess. But resilience becomes dangerous when it is used to excuse failure. A country should not constantly ask its citizens to be stronger when the real problem is that the system keeps making life unnecessarily difficult.

We have normalized things that should never have become normal. A young graduate can spend years without finding meaningful employment and still be told to “keep trying.” A family can struggle to afford basic food and be told that things are hard for everyone. A student can face unbearable financial pressure and be expected to simply endure it.

At some point, we have to stop confusing endurance with progress.

The danger of normalizing suffering is that it lowers our expectations. When people become accustomed to hardship, they begin to see decent living as a luxury rather than a right. They stop asking why things are this way and start asking only how they can survive another day.

This is how a society can become trapped. People become too busy surviving to demand change, while those responsible for creating better systems face less pressure to do so.

Nigeria does not need citizens who are merely good at surviving. Nigerians deserve a country where survival is not the greatest achievement available to ordinary people.

Perhaps the real question is not why Nigerians are so resilient. Perhaps we should ask why Nigerians have been forced to become so resilient in the first place.
