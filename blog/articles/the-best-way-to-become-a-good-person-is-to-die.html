The Best Way to Become a Good Person Is to Die

November 2, 2026 · Churchill A. Bighan

A son who spends his whole life disrespecting his parents, and who drinks or smokes three times a day before he even eats his three square meals.

When he eventually dies of liver cirrhosis or heart failure at the age of 28, everybody at his burial will remember that he was a very good boy. Even his mother, who once told him she regretted giving birth to him, will loudly cry, telling everyone that he had a good heart.

Suddenly, everybody has a different story. His friends describe him as generous, his neighbours remember his kindness, and people who complained about him for years are now competing to describe his good character.

Wait a minute. Are we talking about the same person?

Apparently, death comes with an automatic public relations department.

Consider the businessman who refuses to pay his employees for months. When he dies, those same employees are expected to contribute towards his burial. Suddenly, he was a philanthropist who touched countless lives.

Even politicians are not left out. A man who spent years making promises he never fulfilled can receive a burial tribute describing him as a selfless servant of humanity. Apparently, even abandoned projects are expected to respect the dead.

Perhaps there is a name for this peculiar tradition. The Romans had an expression: De mortuis nil nisi bonum — speak nothing but good of the dead.

But surely, respecting the dead should not require rewriting their entire history.

Perhaps we should learn to appreciate people while they are alive, rather than waiting until they can no longer hear us to tell the world how wonderful they were.

Dying gives you free PR. Apparently, death is the most affordable reputation management strategy available to mankind.
