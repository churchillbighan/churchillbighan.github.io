Why Nigerian Parents Think Suffering Builds Character

October 22, 2026

Written by Churchill A. Bighan

There is a popular belief in many Nigerian homes that if a child does not suffer, that child will become spoiled. Suffering is presented as a teacher, hardship as preparation for life, and struggle as something every young person must experience before becoming successful.

But there is a problem with this mindset: suffering does not automatically build character.

Sometimes, suffering simply causes suffering.

Many Nigerian parents grew up in difficult circumstances. They experienced shortages, walked long distances, endured strict discipline and learned to survive without many of the comforts available today. Naturally, they want their children to develop the same resilience. But somewhere along the line, resilience became confused with unnecessary hardship.

A child can be taught discipline without being denied basic comfort. A young person can learn responsibility without being deliberately subjected to deprivation. Failure can teach valuable lessons, but there is no reason to manufacture failure simply because someone believes struggle is good for the soul.

Yet in some Nigerian homes, asking for something as simple as better food, a comfortable environment or financial support can produce the familiar response: “When I was your age, I suffered more than this.”

That statement may be true, but it does not necessarily make the suffering necessary.

One generation surviving hardship should not become a reason for the next generation to repeat it.

Perhaps this is one reason some Nigerians have developed an unusual relationship with suffering. We sometimes praise people for enduring conditions that should actually be questioned. A student struggling without basic necessities is told to “be strong.” A young graduate unable to find work is told to “keep hustling.” Someone working under terrible conditions is told to be grateful because “at least you have a job.”

There is certainly value in learning resilience. Life will never be completely comfortable, and young people need to understand responsibility, delayed gratification and perseverance.

But there is a difference between preparing someone for hardship and deliberately making their life harder.

Parents should want their children to become stronger, not simply more accustomed to suffering.

The goal of raising a child should not be to ensure that they suffer exactly as much as their parents did. It should be to give them the wisdom and character to build a life that is better than the one they inherited.

If your child succeeds without suffering as much as you did, that should not be a disappointment. It should be the evidence that you succeeded as a parent.
