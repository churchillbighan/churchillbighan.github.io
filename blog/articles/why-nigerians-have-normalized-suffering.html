Why Nigerians Have Normalized Suffering

October 22, 2026

There is something deeply disturbing about the way Nigerians have learned to live with suffering. What should outrage us has gradually become ordinary. What should be considered unacceptable is often dismissed with a simple phrase: "That is Nigeria."

Nigerians struggle with rising food prices, unreliable electricity, poor roads, unemployment and an increasingly expensive cost of living. Yet many have become experts at adjusting to conditions that should never have been acceptable in the first place.

We celebrate survival so much that we sometimes forget to question why survival has become the standard.

There is nothing wrong with resilience. Nigerians are resourceful, hardworking and remarkably determined. But resilience becomes dangerous when it is used to excuse failure. A country should not constantly ask its citizens to be stronger when the systems around them should be working to make life better.

A graduate can spend years searching for meaningful employment and be told to keep trying. A family can struggle to afford food and be told that times are hard for everyone. A student can face overwhelming financial pressure and be expected to endure it silently.

At some point, we must stop confusing endurance with progress.

The danger of normalizing suffering is that it lowers our expectations. When hardship becomes familiar, people may begin to see decent living as a luxury rather than something they have a legitimate reason to demand. Instead of asking why conditions remain poor, they concentrate on how to survive another day.

A society cannot make meaningful progress if its people are permanently occupied with survival. Citizens need the time, resources and freedom to pursue education, build businesses, support their families and hold institutions accountable.

Nigeria does not need citizens who are merely good at surviving. Nigerians deserve a country where survival is not the greatest achievement available to ordinary people.

Perhaps the real question is not why Nigerians are so resilient. Perhaps we should ask why Nigerians have been forced to become so resilient in the first place.
