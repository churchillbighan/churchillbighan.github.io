Stop Calling It Hausa-Fulani Insecurity: The Hausa People Need to Wake Up!

WRITTEN BY CHURCHILL A. BIGHAN
November 16, 2026

Imagine a Hausa farmer in Zamfara waking up one morning, only to discover that his village has been surrounded by armed men. His crops are destroyed, his neighbours are kidnapped and his family is forced to pay millions in ransom.

Now imagine that after all this, someone comes on social media and describes the situation as a Hausa-Fulani problem.

Interesting, isn't it?

The man who lost his farm is Hausa. Many of the people being kidnapped are Hausa Muslims. Yet, a significant number of the armed groups responsible for these attacks are predominantly Fulani.

So why are we so uncomfortable having this conversation?

Let's get something straight. Hausa and Fulani are two different ethnic groups. They have interacted for centuries, share religion, intermarry and have considerable cultural similarities. But they are not the same people.

And I think the Hausa people need to start recognising this distinction.

Consider the history. Before Usman dan Fodio's jihad in 1804, Hausaland already had powerful kingdoms, established political institutions and thriving commercial centres. Kano, Katsina, Gobir, Zazzau and Daura had their own histories and rulers.

Then came the jihad. Several Hausa ruling dynasties were defeated, and a new political order emerged under the Sokoto Caliphate.

But today, sometimes when Northern Nigerian history is discussed, you would almost think the region had no history before Usman dan Fodio. What happened to the Hausa kingdoms? What about Kanem-Borno? Why does our historical conversation sometimes begin and end with the Caliphate?

Now, imagine a Hausa influencer with hundreds of thousands of followers. He can spend three days arguing about international politics, defending religious interests and analysing everything happening in the Middle East. But when bandits kidnap people in Katsina or Zamfara, suddenly, everybody becomes diplomatic.

Why?

Is it because the perpetrators are considered their people? If so, when did ethnic solidarity become more important than the lives of the people being kidnapped?

A Hausa farmer in Zamfara should not have to suffer in silence because the criminals attacking his community share a religion or historical connection with another group.

You can be Muslim without defending every Muslim. You can be Northern without defending every Northerner. And you can be Hausa without accepting responsibility for crimes committed by Fulani bandit groups.

I am not suggesting that every Fulani person is a bandit. That would be ridiculous. Fulani communities are also victims of these criminals. What I am saying is that we must be able to identify the composition of criminal networks without turning the discussion into an ethnic taboo.

Perhaps the first step towards confronting insecurity in Northern Nigeria is admitting that Hausa and Fulani are distinct communities with distinct identities and that neither should be expected to protect the other from legitimate criticism.

The enemy is not an ethnicity. It is the criminal who kidnaps your neighbour, the militia that burns your village and the system that allows them to operate.

And if we cannot even have an honest conversation about that, what exactly are we hoping to solve?
