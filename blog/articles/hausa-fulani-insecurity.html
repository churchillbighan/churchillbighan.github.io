Stop Calling It Hausa-Fulani Insecurity: The Hausa People Need to Wake Up!

WRITTEN BY CHURCHILL A. BIGHAN
November 16, 2026

I think there is a conversation about insecurity in Northern Nigeria that we have deliberately refused to have.

First, can we stop pretending that Hausa and Fulani are the same people?

I find it interesting that whenever banditry is discussed, we suddenly become uncomfortable identifying the ethnic backgrounds of the people involved. Meanwhile, communities are being destroyed, farmers are being kidnapped and families are paying millions in ransom.

Let's look at Northwestern Nigeria. A significant number of the notorious bandit groups operating in Zamfara, Katsina and Sokoto are predominantly Fulani. Interestingly, many of their victims are Hausa Muslims. The same insecurity has affected Christian communities in Southern Kaduna.

So, who exactly are we protecting by constantly hiding behind the expression "Hausa-Fulani"?

The Hausa and Fulani are two different ethnic groups. They share religion, history, cultural influences and, in many cases, family ties. But that doesn't make them the same people.

And I think the Hausa people need to start recognising this distinction.

Consider the history. Before Usman dan Fodio's jihad in 1804, Hausaland already had powerful kingdoms, established political institutions and thriving commercial centres. Kano, Katsina, Gobir, Zazzau and Daura had their own histories.

Then came the jihad. Several Hausa ruling dynasties were defeated, and a new political order emerged under the Sokoto Caliphate.

But today, sometimes when Northern Nigerian history is discussed, you would almost think the region had no history before Usman dan Fodio. What happened to the history of the Hausa kingdoms? What about Kanem-Borno? Why does our historical conversation sometimes begin and end with the Caliphate?

Now, here is where I want to direct my attention to the ordinary Hausa person.

Why is there not a louder conversation among Hausa influencers, intellectuals and public commentators about banditry? Why should condemning criminals become an ethnic controversy?

If a criminal is Fulani, say he is Fulani. If he is Hausa, say he is Hausa. What exactly is the problem? Are we now supposed to pretend that criminals have no ethnic identities because mentioning them might offend somebody?

Being Muslim does not mean you have to defend every Muslim. Being Northern does not mean you have to defend every Northerner.

And being Hausa does not mean you have to carry the burden of crimes committed by Fulani bandit groups.

I think the Hausa people need to reclaim their distinct identity and understand that they can have a completely independent voice in confronting insecurity without feeling the need to speak on behalf of another ethnic group.

This isn't about creating hatred between Hausa and Fulani people. Innocent Fulani communities are victims of banditry too, and they deserve protection.

It is about understanding that ethnic solidarity should never become a hiding place for criminals.

Because, quite frankly, if people cannot distinguish between the victims and perpetrators simply because they belong to historically connected communities, then we have a bigger problem than we think.
