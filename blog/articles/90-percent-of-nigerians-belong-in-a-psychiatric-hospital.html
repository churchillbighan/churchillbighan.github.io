90% of Nigerians Belong in a Psychiatric Hospital, and Nobody Wants to Admit It

Written by Churchill A. Bighan | October 31, 2026

Have you noticed how easily Nigerians lose their temper these days? A little disagreement can turn into a fight. Someone mistakenly brushes against your car, and suddenly there is a full-blown confrontation. People insult strangers, shout at bus conductors, fight over insignificant issues and turn ordinary conversations into competitions of aggression.

Everybody seems angry. Everybody is shouting. Everybody wants to prove a point, and nobody wants to calm down.

But have we ever stopped to ask ourselves why?

Depression and other mental health struggles are real, and while loudness, aggression and short temper are not clinical symptoms of depression on their own, they can sometimes reflect deeper emotional distress.

Consider the pressure Nigerians deal with daily: unpaid salaries, rising food prices, unemployment, insecurity, relationship problems and the constant struggle to survive.

People are mentally exhausted, emotionally frustrated and increasingly impatient with one another. Yet, instead of seeking help, we have normalised aggression. We call it being strong, being street-smart or simply having a Nigerian mentality.

Perhaps Nigerians need therapy more than another motivational speech. We need to learn emotional regulation, recognise psychological distress and stop treating mental health care as something reserved for people who have completely lost control.

And here is my controversial question:

If we can spend millions treating our physical illnesses but cannot spend time or money treating our mental health, are we really a healthy nation, or have we simply become accustomed to being psychologically unwell?
